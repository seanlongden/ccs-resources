# Why Should a Client Choose You Over Another Agency?

If a prospect says, “Another agency does this for less,” explaining that you care more or work harder gives them little to compare. You need to show what they get from working with you, why it matters to their business and why they can believe you can deliver it.

**By the end of this resource, you will have a clear reason for the right buyer to choose you, the evidence behind it and an explanation you can use on a sales call.** You will also know whether you need to change your message, your target buyer or the offer itself.

## Start With the Buying Decision

A service is what you do: cold email, paid ads, SEO or content. An offer explains what a buyer gets, the outcome it is intended to produce, what each side has to do and the terms. Your positioning makes it clear who that offer suits and why they should choose it over their alternatives.

A referral can bring you a client because someone they trust has recommended you. You might never have had to make an explicit case for your offer. A stranger does not arrive with that trust. They need a reason to act, not just a description of your service.

Two agencies can both book meetings but solve different buying problems. One buyer wants an inexpensive source of appointments and has a team to manage everything around them. Another wants suitable sales conversations without having to supervise targeting, chase replies and investigate every appointment.

The second buyer might value a wider service. That does not mean every buyer will, or that you already deliver it. **Your job is to find a valuable difference you can substantiate, or make a deliberate decision to build one.**

## 1. Find Out What Your Buyer Is Trying to Achieve

Start with one type of buyer you can serve well. Use your most relevant successful clients as a starting point, rather than narrowing to an industry at random. This is a starting focus for the exercise, not a permanent ban on serving other clients.

Open your recent sales conversations, client onboarding notes and delivery records. Include wins, losses and stalled deals. Look for the same buying situation across them, not only the quotes that support the answer you want.

For each conversation, record:

- **The trigger:** What changed that made them look for help now?
- **The result:** What would a good outcome let them do in their business?
- **The alternative:** Another provider, hiring, doing it themselves or continuing as they are?
- **The concern:** What disappointed them before, or makes this decision feel risky?
- **The evidence:** Their actual words, with a call date, timestamp or note reference.

Ask naturally: “What made you look at this now?”, “What did you try?”, “Where did it break down?” and “If you didn't work with us, what would you do instead?” Do not ask leading questions such as, “Wouldn't it be better if we handled everything?”

**Weak answer:** “SaaS companies want more leads.”

**Useful answer:** “The founder wants more conversations with suitable buyers, but their last campaign left them sorting replies and attending calls with companies they could not serve.” This is only a finding when actual evidence supports it.

If you have little evidence, write “assumption” next to the answer. Speak with people who match your proposed buyer and ask about their experience. Keep unanswered questions visible; do not let AI invent the missing buyer research.

**Workbook output:** A buyer situation, their alternative and the source behind your understanding. Use [Map Out Your ICP](/resources/set-up/map-out-icp) if you need help choosing the starting buyer.

## 2. Identify What Gets in the Way of the Result

Map the journey from hiring you to receiving value. What must happen? Where could the buyer lose time, do unwanted work, wait for progress or doubt the result?

For a meeting-generation service, the journey might include selecting the right companies, making an appealing offer, reaching them, handling interest, confirming fit, scheduling and attending the call. The buyer then has to convert suitable opportunities into business. A booked slot is not a signed client.

Check four questions against the evidence you gathered:

- **Result:** Are we pursuing the outcome they value, or a metric that leaves the real problem unsolved?
- **Confidence:** What would they need to see before trusting us to deliver?
- **Delay:** Where do they wait without useful progress or clarity?
- **Work:** What must they organise, learn, chase or do themselves?

Turn a broad concern into a specific obstacle. “Bad leads” is too vague. “The appointments include companies below our minimum contract size” points towards an observable qualification decision.

**Workbook output:** The important obstacles, why they matter and whether each is confirmed or assumed. Do not promise to remove everything; first understand the problem.

## 3. Develop Ways to Remove Those Obstacles

Take one obstacle at a time. Ask what you already do about it, what you could change and what the buyer would still need to do.

Suppose the concern is, “I don't know why these people are on my calendar.” Possible responses include explaining an existing screening process, agreeing clearer criteria before launch, or supplying the relevant reply and fit notes with each appointment. These are different options with different costs. They are not automatically part of your offer.

For each option, write:

1. **The action:** What specifically happens, who does it and when?
2. **The buyer benefit:** What uncertainty, work or delay does that remove?
3. **The status:** Already delivered, possible improvement or outside our scope?
4. **The evidence:** What shows that it happens or helps?

A valuable difference need not be a technology nobody else has. It can be your experience with this buyer, relevant evidence, the work you take responsibility for or how you make progress visible. But if competing providers do the same thing, do not claim exclusive capability.

### Show the Work You Take Off Their Plate

“We manage campaigns” hides the work. A clearer explanation, **if it matches your service**, is: “We choose the accounts, build the list, write and test the messages, and manage replies through to booking. You approve the audience and offer, then take the sales calls.”

That makes the buyer's remaining job clear. It also explains why the service is useful. A long list of tools or technical tasks does neither on its own.

### Decide What Kind of Problem You Have

- **You already deliver valuable work, but buyers cannot see it:** Improve the explanation and show evidence.
- **The buyer does not care about that value:** Reconsider the buyer or buying situation. Do not force a larger package on someone who only needs a smaller service.
- **You cannot currently solve the important obstacle:** Evaluate a real delivery change before putting it in your pitch.

**Workbook output:** Options with explicit delivery status. Do not jump straight from “buyers worry about this” to a promise you cannot fulfil.

## 4. Choose a Difference You Can Deliver Profitably

Compare your options against the buyer's real alternative. Find out its scope where possible. “The other agency is cheaper” does not tell you what is included, and a lower price is not evidence of worse work.

Choose an option only when you can answer:

- Does this buyer care enough for it to affect their decision?
- What would be different from their alternative?
- Can we deliver it with our current people, time and systems?
- What will it cost, including the founder's time, tools and rework?
- What proof do we have, and what still needs testing?
- What will the client remain responsible for?

For an improvement, estimate delivery hours × realistic hourly cost, plus tools and other direct costs. Compare that with the fee and your required margin. A low-cash-cost extra can still consume a lot of your time. Use [Your Service Model & Pricing](/resources/set-up/service-model-pricing) for the wider pricing decision.

**Adding every possible service is not the goal.** Removing one important obstacle well can matter more than a pile of bonuses. Training, system ownership, extra channels and new hires belong in the offer only when they serve the chosen buyer and make commercial sense.

A guarantee can reduce a buyer's concern when you can honour it. Define the promised outcome, deadline, responsibilities and remedy accurately. Do not present a guarantee as proof that an untested delivery process works, or claim that the buyer has no risk at all.

**Workbook output:** The options to keep, change, test or reject, with reasons. Select the few that make the strongest supported case. If no meaningful difference is established yet, label your position a hypothesis and name the comparison or delivery test needed before using it as a claim.

## 5. Turn Your Decisions Into a Reason to Choose You

Now write the explanation. Put the buyer's desired result first. Then explain the valuable work, relevant evidence and remaining responsibilities that make the offer believable.

Use this as a drafting aid, not a sentence you must recite:

> For [buyer in a specific situation], we [concrete work] so they can [desired result] without [work or obstacle we genuinely remove]. We can show [relevant evidence]. They remain responsible for [their part].

Avoid turning intentions into guarantees. A process demonstration can show how you work. A comparable client result can support a performance claim. Neither justifies promising the same result to every buyer.

### Match the Evidence to the Concern

If they doubt fit, show the criteria and an appropriately anonymised example. If they doubt your ability to deliver, show relevant results with scope and dates. If they worry about workload, walk through who does what. General experience is useful context, but it is not proof of a particular promised result.

If you have no results yet, say what you can demonstrate and what remains unproven. A sample or pilot can help you learn; it must not be presented as a successful client case.

### Use the Same Reason in Different Places

- **Outreach:** Give this buyer a credible reason to reply. The first email does not need the whole proposal.
- **Sales call:** Diagnose whether the buying situation applies, then explain the relevant difference and show evidence.
- **Proposal:** Confirm the result sought, scope, responsibilities, proof and terms you actually agreed.

Read the [worked example](/resources/sales/positioning-worked-example) for an introduction, a cheaper-provider response and a proposal summary built from the same decisions.

**Workbook output:** Your positioning brief, the proof you will use and the words you will say. If your answer is still “better quality”, name the specific difference a buyer would experience.

## 6. Test Whether Buyers Understand and Value It

Use the explanation with suitable prospects. Ask them to describe what they think you would do and what they would still need to handle. Then listen to what they question and compare.

In your normal sales notes or tracker, record the buyer situation, the explanation used, their response, the evidence requested and the agreed next step. Include conversations that do not progress.

Use the pattern to decide what to do next:

- **They misunderstand the scope:** Make the explanation clearer.
- **They understand but do not value the difference:** Recheck the buyer or the obstacle you chose.
- **They value it but doubt you:** Strengthen relevant proof or reduce the size of the unproven commitment.
- **They want it but delivery is not viable:** Revisit scope, price or how the work is done.
- **They understand, value it and move forward:** Keep learning from comparable conversations before changing the position.

A polite compliment is not the same as a buying decision. One rejection does not establish a trend, and a better message cannot fix every sales problem.

**Workbook output:** What you learned, the next change and the evidence needed. Bring the completed brief to coaching so the discussion starts with your decisions, not a blank page.

## Put It Into Practice

**Your finished brief should let another person explain:** who this is for, what they get, why it is preferable for that buyer, what supports the claim and what each side must do.
